## Introduction

Most new products never earn the word innovation. This report argues that innovation is proven in use, not at launch, and tests that claim on two attempts to automate the last mile of delivery. Zipline's Platform 2 (P2) drone system reached paying customers in 2025 and is now scaling with Walmart and Uber. FedEx's Roxo delivery robot was unveiled on national television in 2019 and cancelled in 2022 without a commercial customer. Both tackled the same problem with capable technology, so the contrast isolates how each innovation was managed.

Part 1 sets out my definition of innovation and the theory and experience behind it. Part 2 analyses the two cases using desirability, feasibility and viability, the Double Diamond, dynamic capabilities and the tension between exploration and exploitation. Part 3 turns the findings into recommendations for a hypothetical established logistics company, covering its process, the behaviour of individual employees and the actions of senior leaders.

@@part1
## Part 1: My definition of innovation

### How I define innovation

Innovation is the process of turning a new idea into something that people actually adopt and that can sustain itself. Novelty is only the starting point. An innovation is proven in use: it solves a real problem for its users and creates enough value for the organisation to keep providing it.

### Justification and explanation

My definition rests on three ideas. First, implementation separates innovation from creativity. The Oslo Manual requires a new product to have been 'made available to potential users' (OECD and Eurostat, 2018, p. 20), and this unit defines innovation as the implementation of value-adding ideas. Second, adoption is the test. Rogers (2003) shows that an idea spreads only when users judge it better than what it replaces, so an unveiled prototype is not yet an innovation. Third, value must flow both ways. IDEO's three lenses ask whether people want it, whether it can be built and whether it pays (Brown, 2009), and Tidd and Bessant (2021) describe innovation as creating value from ideas. 'Sustain itself' reflects that last lens.

The definition has a limit. It can undervalue experiments that fail but teach, which March (1991) treats as necessary exploration, so I apply it to outcomes and not to effort.

My internships shaped this view. Auditing assets before a restructuring taught me to separate what is evidenced from what is asserted. ## Part 2: Case study analysis

### How I judge success and failure

An innovation succeeds when the users it was built for adopt it, it works reliably at scale, and its provider has a credible way to keep supplying it. It fails when it falls short of the adoption its sponsor expected within the time and money the sponsor was willing to commit. Table 1 applies the three lenses to both cases.

### A. Successful innovation: Zipline Platform 2

**The innovation.** Zipline, a Californian company, had spent seven years delivering medical supplies by fixed-wing drone, beginning in Rwanda (Zipline, 2023; Sequoia Capital, n.d.). Platform 2, unveiled in March 2023, is its home-delivery system. An aircraft hovers more than 300 feet up and lowers a small tethered 'droid' that places a 6–8 lb order on a doorstep within a 10-mile radius (Zipline, 2023). It promises customers delivery in minutes and gives retailers an alternative to sending a car and driver.

**Process: a complete Double Diamond.** P2's development followed the Design Council's (2019) four stages. In Discover, the team learned that the first platform's parachute drop needed open space and could not serve dense housing (IEEE Spectrum, 2024). In Define, they framed the problem as precise delivery to a doorstep, not as a better drone; the chief executive says the aircraft is only 15% of the system (Sequoia Capital, n.d.). In Develop, a quick winch prototype failed in coastal wind, which led to the powered droid, and the team deleted a fast approach path after seeing people back away from it (IEEE Spectrum, 2024). In Deliver, Zipline ran more than 9,000 test flights a week (Watson, 2025) and began in one town before expanding. Evidence about users shaped the technology, and prototypes were cheap enough to fail. One caution applies: this account relies on interviews given by Zipline's own staff after the event, so it is probably tidier than the reality.

**Internal factors: dynamic capabilities.** Teece (2007) argues that lasting advantage comes from sensing opportunities, seizing them and transforming the organisation. Zipline sensed that its first platform was 'profitable, but it wasn't large enough' (Sequoia Capital, n.d.). It seized by committing the company: 'If Platform Two failed, Zipline would've failed' (Sequoia Capital, n.d.). It then transformed, expanding manufacturing and adding channels through Walmart and Uber Eats (Zipline, 2026; Korosec, 2026b). Leaders protected a long, risky exploration instead of relying on the existing business.

**External factors.** Regulation and partners helped. The FAA authorised Zipline to fly beyond visual line of sight without observers (FAA, 2023), and Walmart, a partner since 2021, supplied stores as launch sites (Korosec, 2026a). The sequence also fits Moore's (2014) advice to cross the chasm from a beachhead: Zipline proved its service in healthcare, where the need was urgent, before approaching mainstream retail customers who buy on proven results.

**How far is it a success?** The evidence is strong on desirability and feasibility but incomplete on viability. P2 reached customers about a year later than first indicated (Bellan, 2023; Sequoia Capital, n.d.). Zipline is private, publishes no cost or profit figures, and reports its own growth numbers. Residents in some areas object to noise and privacy risks (Korosec, 2026b). I therefore judge P2 a successful market entry whose economics are not yet proven.

**Lessons.** Start from a defined user problem. Make prototypes cheap enough to fail. Protect exploration until evidence arrives. Treat regulators and partners as part of the design.

### B. Failed innovation: FedEx Roxo

**The innovation.** Roxo, the FedEx SameDay Bot, was an autonomous sidewalk robot developed with DEKA, the maker of the iBot powered wheelchair (FedEx, 2019). It was meant to carry same-day orders from shops to nearby homes, on the reasoning that 'more than 60 percent of merchants' customers live within three miles of a store location' (FedEx, 2019). FedEx unveiled it on The Tonight Show in February 2019. In October 2022 an internal memo ended the programme because it 'did not meet necessary near-term value requirements' (Contreras, 2022). I found no record of a paying customer.

**Process: failure in Define.** Roxo ran the first diamond backwards. The solution existed before the problem was specified: the robot was shown on television and only then scheduled for its first street tests (FedEx, 2019). Six retailers were named, but only 'to help assess retailers' autonomous delivery needs' (FedEx, 2019). FedEx did some discovery, yet it never converged on a brief stating which customer, which job and what result would count as success. This is the pattern the unit calls falling in love with the technology, not the problem. The consequences followed. New York City ordered the robots off its streets (Korosec, 2019). Commercial pilots hoped for in summer 2020 did not appear (Baker, 2020), and in 2021 FedEx was still announcing a 'first appearance' abroad (FedEx, 2021). Because success was never defined, a cost programme eventually defined it. A written brief in 2019, naming one retailer, one delivery job and a date by which that retailer would pay, would have exposed the gap years earlier.

**Internal factors: exploration starved by exploitation.** March (1991) shows that exploitation's returns are near and certain while exploration's are distant and uncertain, so rational budgeting favours the core. O'Reilly and Tushman (2004) recommend a separate, protected unit with senior sponsorship. Roxo appears to have had neither a ring-fenced budget nor agreed stage gates. On 22 September 2022 FedEx announced cost savings of $2.2–2.7 billion (FedEx, 2022); about two weeks later Roxo was gone. The decision may well have been right. The failure is that it took more than three and a half years and a profit crisis to reach it. FedEx would frame the outcome differently. Its memo said Roxo 'served a valuable purpose: to rapidly advance our understanding and use of robotic technology' (Contreras, 2022). Learning did occur, but against my definition, and against FedEx's own launch promise of same-day delivery for retailers, the innovation failed.

**External factors.** Roxo was not alone. Amazon ended field tests of its Scout robot the same week (Heater, 2022), and one operator argues that sidewalk robots need 'a lot of scale and … a lot of density' (Garland, 2023), a point that academic cost modelling supports (Lemardelé et al., 2021). Sector immaturity explains part of the outcome, but not why years of testing produced no customer.

**Lessons.** Define the customer, the measure of success and the exit conditions before building. Prove demand with one paying customer before publicity. Fund exploration through protected, staged commitments so that projects end on evidence.

### Comparing the cases

Table 2 sets the cases side by side. The clearest difference is sequence. Zipline moved from problem to brief to prototype to customer, while FedEx moved from solution to publicity to tests. The comparison has limits. It rests on two cases read with hindsight, the companies differ in age and funding, and drones answer to one national regulator while sidewalk robots face many cities and states. The frameworks organise the evidence; they do not prove cause. Even so, both cases point the same way: the earlier a team fixes its problem, its measures and its exit, the cheaper its mistakes become.

## Part 3: Recommendations for future innovation

These recommendations are for a hypothetical organisation: an established, mid-sized parcel carrier that wants to automate local delivery. Like FedEx it has a profitable core, cautious budgeting and little habit of experiment. Zipline shows what to copy and Roxo what to avoid.

@@process
### Process recommendations: the Double Diamond

The Double Diamond can look linear, although the Design Council (2019) stresses iteration. Each stage below therefore ends with an output and a decision that can send the team back.

**Discover: study the problem before the technology.** Give a small cross-functional team six weeks and no solution. Members should ride with drivers, sit with retail customers who place same-day orders and interview at least twenty recipients. They should also meet the people who can stop the project: city transport officials, insurers and residents' groups. Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten (2013) call this anticipation and inclusion. Roxo shows the cost of skipping it, because FedEx heard from New York's regulators only after its robot appeared on the street (Korosec, 2019). The team should use the five whys to move from symptoms, such as 'same-day delivery is expensive', to causes. Zipline's key insight, that parachute drops could not serve dense housing, came from operating experience and not from a technology roadmap (IEEE Spectrum, 2024). Because teams tend to hear what confirms their idea, one member should be asked to collect evidence against it. Output: a ranked list of evidenced problems, each with the number of customers affected.

**Define: write the brief and the exit before building.** This is where Roxo failed, so it deserves the most discipline. The team should convert the top problem into a one-page brief containing a 'how might we' question, one beachhead customer segment (Moore, 2014) and a statement, in numbers, of what desirable, feasible and viable would look like. Every claim in the brief should be sorted into known, assumed or unknown, as the unit recommends, and the assumed column becomes the test plan. The measures should be concrete, for example 'five retailers each pay for one hundred deliveries within six months, at a price that covers the cost per delivery'. If several problems compete, the unit's RICE score, which weighs reach, impact and confidence against effort, gives a transparent way to choose. The team should then agree exit criteria with the sponsor: the evidence that would end the project at each stage. Holgersson et al. (2026) advise designing the exit at entry, before reputations are attached to continuing. Finally, a pre-mortem (Klein, 2007) asks the team to imagine the project cancelled in three years and to list why. Had FedEx done this, 'no retailer will pay' and 'cities will not permit it' could have been on the page in 2019. Output: a signed brief with measures and exit conditions.

**Develop: build cheaply and test the riskiest assumption first.** The team should prototype to learn, not to impress. The first prototypes should take days and test the assumption most likely to kill the idea, which is usually demand and not engineering. The carrier could offer retailers a 'robot delivery' option that is fulfilled by a person with a trolley, to see whether anyone pays before a robot exists. Blank (2013) describes this as getting out of the building. Tests should involve real users in real conditions. Zipline's winch failed in wind at its test site, and watching people step back led the team to delete an approach path that worked technically (IEEE Spectrum, 2024). Decisions should stay reversible while evidence is thin, which is the unit's distinction between two-way and one-way doors. A national launch is a one-way door, and Roxo went through it on television before its first street test. One limit must be respected. 'Fail fast' suits software better than machines on public streets, so safety-critical tests belong on private sites first, and the unit's Facebook Libra example shows that moving fast and breaking things does not guarantee success. Output: evidence that moves items from assumed to known, or a decision to return to Define.

**Deliver: start narrow with paying customers and scale by gates.** The carrier should launch in one town with one retailer that pays. Zipline's first P2 customer delivery was in a single Arkansas town (Sequoia Capital, n.d.), and when Walmart signed for 300 more sites the two companies had only 21 active ones (Korosec, 2026a). Funding should be released in stages against the measures agreed in Define, as in Cooper's (1990) stage gates and the metered funding of Osterwalder et al. (2020). The team should track all three lenses, including cost per delivery, because adoption without proven viability is the gap that remains in Zipline's own case. External risk needs deliberate management. Kaplan and Mikes (2012) separate preventable, strategy and external risks; regulation here is external, so the carrier should plan scenarios and build relationships with regulators early. Finally, adoption inside the firm must be planned. Drivers and depot managers may resist automation, and Kotter and Schlesinger (2008) recommend education, participation and negotiation over imposition. What is learned in the first town should start a new round of Discover, because scaling reveals problems that a pilot hides. Output: a go, hold or stop decision at each gate, made on evidence.

### Individual behaviour recommendations

A process works only if individuals feed it honest information. Six behaviours matter most.

**1. Fall in love with the problem.** Every employee on an innovation project should be able to state, in one sentence, whose problem it solves. When they cannot, they should say so. This is the simplest defence against the first of the unit's five pitfalls, working on the wrong problem. Zipline's engineers showed the attitude when they treated the aircraft as a small part of a delivery service. A practical routine is to spend time each month with customers or front-line staff, and to stay with the problem longer than feels comfortable, since the unit warns that quick solutions are often uncreative.

**2. Separate what is known from what is assumed.** Innovators become attached to their ideas and read weak signals as proof. The habit to build is asking 'what is the evidence, and who has checked it?' My audit internship is my reference point. **3. Speak up early, especially with bad news.** Edmondson (1999) shows that teams learn when members feel safe to raise problems, and a meta-analysis confirms the link with performance (Frazier et al., 2017). Voice is also an individual choice. Vuori and Huy (2016) found that Nokia's middle managers understood the smartphone threat but softened it for their superiors. Each employee should practise reporting problems plainly and early, with evidence and a proposed next step, and should thank colleagues who do the same.

**4. Experiment small and treat failure as data.** Employees should prefer a test they can run this week to a plan they can perfect this quarter. The unit's advice is to make failure cheap, legible and classified: small enough to afford, written down so that others learn, and labelled so that a failed experiment is not treated like negligence. After a test aircraft made a parachute landing, Zipline's engineers submitted a fix within 17 hours and later published the analysis (Watson, 2025). The difference between failure and learning is whether the result was recorded and changed the next decision.

**6. Seek out people who think differently.** A problem has more sides than one person can see. Innovators should invite colleagues from operations, finance, legal and customer service into early discussions and treat disagreement about the task as useful. Belbin's (1981) team roles give a simple vocabulary for noticing which contributions a group lacks. The aim is diversity of perspective combined with shared standards.

These behaviours have a limit. Schein (1984) shows that people follow what the organisation actually rewards, and the unit describes culture as the residue of what is rewarded and punished. Individuals can start these habits, but they last only if leaders make them safe.

@@leadership
### Leadership recommendations

Senior leaders decide whether the process and behaviours above survive contact with the budget. Six actions follow from the cases.

**1. Ring-fence exploration.** Because the core business has better data and nearer returns, it wins every fair contest for resources (March, 1991). Leaders should therefore set an explicit portfolio, for example a 70-20-10 split, and protect the exploratory share for a fixed period that cannot be raided in a difficult quarter. The split is a rule of thumb, and the unit notes that even Google applied it unevenly. What matters is the protection. Roxo was cut about two weeks after a cost programme was announced (FedEx, 2022; Contreras, 2022). Zipline's leaders kept funding P2 through a year of delay, knowing that its failure would end the company (Sequoia Capital, n.d.).

**2. Separate the unit and integrate at the top.** O'Reilly and Tushman (2004) found that exploratory units succeed when they have their own team, measures and culture but report to a senior executive who also owns the core. The carrier should create a small autonomy unit outside the operating divisions, with a named executive sponsor and a deputy, so that the project does not depend on one champion.

**3. Fund in stages against evidence agreed in advance.** Protection must not become a blank cheque. Leaders should release money at gates tied to the measures in the Define brief (Cooper, 1990; Osterwalder et al., 2020) and close projects that miss them. This makes stopping normal and cheap. Roxo's cancellation was probably correct, but a gate in 2020 that required one paying retailer could have reached the same answer two years earlier.

**4. Reward bad news.** Leaders set the price of honesty. If the first person to report a failed test is blamed, reporting stops, as it did at Nokia (Vuori and Huy, 2016). Leaders should ask for problems in every review, respond with curiosity, and promote people who surface risks early. They should model the behaviour by describing their own mistakes. Schein (1984) implies that posters and value statements will not achieve this; what changes behaviour is who is promoted and what happens to the bearer of bad news. The evidence here deserves care. Google's Project Aristotle, often cited for psychological safety, was never peer reviewed, but Edmondson (1999) and Frazier et al. (2017) provide stronger support.

**5. Engage regulators and communities as partners.** In delivery automation the right to operate is granted by others. Leaders should meet regulators before launch, publish safety data and listen to residents. Zipline had seven years of operations behind it when it unveiled P2 and later won wider flight permissions (Zipline, 2023; FAA, 2023). FedEx received a cease-and-desist order (Korosec, 2019).

**6. Measure what you want more of.** Leaders get what they count. 3M requires that 30% of annual sales come from products introduced in the last four years, a unit example of a target that keeps renewal on the agenda. For early projects the carrier should count leading indicators: assumptions tested, time to the first paying customer and projects stopped at a gate.

**Costs and trade-offs.** These actions are not free. Ring-fencing lowers short-term margins, and leaders must defend that to investors. The carrier must also stop some familiar habits: announcing projects before there is evidence, and funding exploration through the same annual budget round as the core. Stage gates need the discipline to stop popular projects. A separate unit can become isolated from the core, so that its lessons transfer poorly (O'Reilly and Tushman, 2004); the carrier should rotate operational staff through it and review the portfolio annually. Nor do these actions guarantee success. Roxo might have failed even with a perfect process, because the sector was immature. They do make it likely that failure is found early and cheaply.

## Conclusion

This report defined innovation as a new idea that people adopt and that can sustain itself, and the two cases support that test. Zipline's P2 and FedEx's Roxo addressed the same problem with comparable ambition. Zipline defined a user problem, prototyped cheaply and protected a risky exploration until customers confirmed it, although its economics remain unproven. FedEx announced a solution, never fixed what success meant, and let a cost programme decide. The difference lay less in technology than in management, and most of all in the Define stage.

For an established organisation the recommendations reduce to three commitments. Define the problem and the conditions for success and exit before building. Test with cheap prototypes and paying customers before publicity. Give exploration protected, staged funding with senior sponsorship. None of these guarantees success. Together they make it likely that a good idea reaches its users and that a weak one is stopped early at low cost.
