## Introduction

Most new products never earn the word innovation. This report argues that innovation is proven in use, not at launch, and tests that claim on two attempts to automate the last mile of delivery. Zipline's Platform 2 (P2) drone system reached paying customers in 2025 and is now scaling with Walmart and Uber. FedEx's Roxo delivery robot was unveiled on national television in 2019 and cancelled in 2022 while still in pilots. Both tackled the same problem with capable technology, so the contrast isolates how each innovation was managed.

Part 1 defines innovation. Part 2 analyses the two cases with four tools from the unit. Part 3 turns the findings into recommendations for a hypothetical logistics company: its process, its employees' behaviour and its leaders' actions.

@@part1
## Part 1: My definition of innovation

### How I define innovation

Innovation is turning a new idea into something people actually use. A new idea alone is not innovation. It becomes innovation when it solves a real problem for real users, and when it brings enough value to the organisation behind it to keep it going.

### Justification and explanation

My definition rests on three ideas. First, implementation separates innovation from creativity. The Oslo Manual requires a new product to have been 'made available to potential users' (OECD and Eurostat, 2018, p. 20), and this unit defines innovation as the implementation of value-adding ideas. Second, adoption is the test. Tidd and Bessant (2021, p. 19) call invention 'only the first step in a long process of bringing a good idea to widespread and effective use', so an unveiled prototype is not yet an innovation. Third, value must flow both ways. The three lenses used in this unit ask whether people want it, whether it can be built and whether it pays (Osterwalder et al., 2020, p. 19), and Tidd and Bessant define innovation as 'the process of turning ideas into reality and capturing value from them' (p. 22).

The definition has a limit. It can undervalue experiments that fail, although March (1991, p. 71) treats experimentation as essential, so I apply it to outcomes and not to effort.

My internships shaped this view. In the audit of Liaoning Jincheng Petrochemical before its asset restructuring, a figure counted only once it had been checked against evidence. In ICBC's international business department, I learned how firmly a large organisation depends on fixed procedures. In the marketing centre of SIASUN, a robotics manufacturer, the work sat between engineers and the customers who must find the robots useful. Together these taught me to judge an idea by evidence and by use.

## Part 2: Case study analysis

### How I judge success and failure

An innovation succeeds when the users it was built for adopt it, it works reliably at scale, and its provider has a credible way to keep supplying it. It fails when it falls short of the adoption its sponsor expected within the time and money the sponsor was willing to commit. Table 1 applies the three lenses to both cases.

### A. Successful innovation: Zipline Platform 2

**The innovation.** Zipline, a Californian company, had spent seven years delivering medical supplies by fixed-wing drone, beginning in Rwanda (Zipline, 2023; Sequoia Capital, 2025). Platform 2, unveiled in March 2023, is its home-delivery system. An aircraft hovers more than 300 feet up and lowers a small tethered 'droid' that places a 6–8 lb order on a doorstep within a 10-mile radius (Zipline, 2023). It promises customers delivery in minutes and gives retailers an alternative to sending a car and driver.

**Process: a complete Double Diamond.** P2's development can be read through the Design Council's (n.d.) four stages. In Discover, customers had been asking for years, and lidar survey data showed that the first platform's drop zone could reach only 'a bit more than half the market' (Ackerman, 2024). In Define, the team framed the task as precise delivery to the home, not as a better drone. The chief executive had learned in Rwanda that 'the vehicle is only 15% of the complexity of the problem' (Sequoia Capital, 2025). In Develop, a quick winch prototype was blown about by the wind, and the team deleted a faster approach because it 'looks scary' (Ackerman, 2024). In Deliver, the first customer delivery came in Pea Ridge, Arkansas, in January 2025 (Sequoia Capital, 2025), and the team 'now routinely completes more than 9,000 test flights a week' (Watson, 2025). Evidence about users shaped the technology, and prototypes were cheap enough to fail. This account relies on Zipline's own staff, so it is probably tidier than reality.

**Internal factors: dynamic capabilities.** Teece (2007) argues that lasting advantage requires sensing opportunities, seizing them and reconfiguring the firm's assets. Zipline sensed the limit of its first platform, which its investor Alfred Lin describes as 'profitable, but it wasn't large enough' for the team's ambition (Sequoia Capital, 2025). It seized by committing the company, in the chief executive's words 'If Platform Two failed, Zipline would've failed' (Sequoia Capital, 2025), and by investing in channels through Walmart and Uber Eats (Korosec, 2026b). It reconfigured by transferring capability 'from one market to another' (Teece, 2007, p. 1336): from medical logistics in Rwanda to retail delivery in the United States. Leaders protected a long, risky exploration instead of relying on the existing business.

**External factors.** Regulation and partners helped. In 2023 the FAA authorised Zipline's first-generation drone to fly beyond visual line of sight without observers around Salt Lake City (FAA, 2023). Walmart, a partner since 2021 (Korosec, 2026a), is adding the service at its existing stores (Walmart, 2026). The sequence also fits Moore's (2014) beachhead strategy: Zipline proved its service in healthcare before approaching mainstream retail customers.

**How far is it a success?** The evidence is strong on desirability and feasibility but incomplete on viability. The first customer delivery came about a year after the 'early next year' that Zipline indicated in 2023 (Bellan, 2023; Sequoia Capital, 2025). Zipline is private; I found no published cost or profit figures, and its growth numbers are self-reported. Drone delivery in general also faces residents' objections about noise, safety and privacy (Korosec, 2026b). I therefore judge P2 a successful market entry whose economics are not yet proven.

**Lessons.** Start from a defined user problem. Make prototypes cheap enough to fail. Protect exploration until evidence arrives. Treat regulators and partners as part of the design.

### B. Failed innovation: FedEx Roxo

**The innovation.** The FedEx SameDay Bot, later named Roxo, was an autonomous sidewalk robot developed with DEKA, the maker of the iBot powered wheelchair (FedEx, 2019). It was meant to carry same-day orders from shops to nearby homes, on the reasoning that 'more than 60 percent of merchants' customers live within three miles of a store location' (FedEx, 2019). FedEx showed a prototype on The Tonight Show in February 2019. In October 2022 an internal memo ended the programme because it 'did not meet necessary near-term value requirements' (Contreras, 2022). By then Roxo had made deliveries in pilots in the United States and overseas (Contreras, 2022), but I found no report of a commercial launch or a paying customer.

**Process: failure in Define.** My reading is that Roxo's first diamond was never closed. FedEx showed a prototype on television on 26 February 2019 and announced the next day that testing would begin that summer, 'pending final city approvals' (FedEx, 2019). Retailers had seen the prototype and given feedback, but they were named only 'to help assess retailers' autonomous delivery needs' (FedEx, 2019), and none was announced as a customer. The public record contains no brief stating which customer, which job and what result would count as success. This resembles the pattern the unit calls falling in love with the technology, not the problem. FedEx took the robots to New York for a promotional event without permission and received a cease-and-desist order (Korosec, 2019). In January 2020 it still only 'hopes to partner with retailers on commercial pilots' (Baker, 2020), and in July 2021 it announced Roxo's 'first appearance in Asia Pacific' (FedEx, 2021). Because success was not defined at the start, a cost programme supplied the definition at the end. A brief in 2019 naming one retailer, one job and a payment date would have exposed the gap years earlier.

**Internal factors: exploration starved by exploitation.** March (1991, p. 85) argues that exploitation's returns are 'positive, proximate, and predictable' while exploration's are 'uncertain, distant, and often negative', so organisations learn to favour the core. Teece (2007, p. 1327) adds that established product lines can 'starve the new business of financial capital'. O'Reilly and Tushman (2004) therefore recommend separate units integrated at senior-team level. Nothing in the public record suggests that Roxo had a ring-fenced budget or agreed stage gates. On 22 September 2022 FedEx announced cost savings of $2.2–2.7 billion (FedEx, 2022). About two weeks later Roxo was eliminated 'as part of an internal organizational program called DRIVE' (Contreras, 2022). The decision may well have been right. The failure is that it took more than three and a half years and a cost-cutting programme to reach it. FedEx's memo claimed that Roxo had advanced its understanding of robotics (Contreras, 2022). Learning occurred, but against my definition the innovation failed.

**External factors.** Roxo was not alone. Amazon scaled back its Scout delivery robot in the same week (Contreras, 2022; Heater, 2022), and one operator argues that sidewalk robots need 'a lot of scale and … a lot of density' (Garland, 2023). Sector immaturity explains part of the outcome, but not why years of pilots produced no announced customer.

**Lessons.** Define the customer, the measure of success and the exit conditions before building. Prove demand with one paying customer before publicity. Fund exploration through protected, staged commitments so that projects end on evidence.

### Comparing the cases

The clearest difference in Table 2 is sequence. Zipline moved from problem to brief to prototype to customer, while FedEx moved from prototype to publicity to pilots, without a published definition of success. The comparison has limits: two cases read with hindsight, companies of different age and funding, and different regulators. The frameworks organise the evidence; they do not prove cause. Even so, both cases point the same way: the earlier a team fixes its problem, its measures and its exit, the cheaper its mistakes become.

## Part 3: Recommendations for future innovation

These recommendations are for a hypothetical organisation: an established, mid-sized parcel carrier that wants to automate local delivery. Like FedEx it has a profitable core, cautious budgeting and little habit of experiment. Zipline shows what to copy and Roxo what to avoid.

@@process
### Process recommendations: the Double Diamond

The Double Diamond can look linear, although the Design Council (n.d.) states that it 'is not a linear process'. Each stage below therefore ends with an output and a decision that can send the team back.

**Discover: study the problem before the technology.** Give a small cross-functional team six weeks and no solution. Members should ride with drivers, sit with retail customers who place same-day orders and interview at least twenty recipients. They should also meet the people who can stop the project: city transport officials, insurers and residents' groups. Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten (2013) call this inclusion, and pair it with anticipation: asking 'what if' questions early. Roxo shows the cost of skipping it: FedEx took its robots to New York for a promotional event without permission and received a cease-and-desist order (Korosec, 2019). The five whys help to move from symptoms to causes. Zipline's key insight came from customers and data, not from a technology roadmap: survey data showed that its first platform could reach only 'a bit more than half the market' (Ackerman, 2024). Because teams are naturally overconfident, independent reviewers should act as devil's advocates (Kaplan and Mikes, 2012). Output: a ranked list of evidenced problems, each with the number of customers affected.

**Define: write the brief and the exit before building.** This is where Roxo failed, so it deserves the most discipline. The team should convert the top problem into a one-page brief containing a 'how might we' question, one beachhead customer segment (Moore, 2014) and a statement, in numbers, of what desirable, feasible and viable would look like. Every claim in the brief should be sorted into known, assumed or unknown, as the unit recommends, and the assumed column becomes the test plan. The measures should be concrete, for example 'five retailers each pay for one hundred deliveries within six months, at a price that covers the cost per delivery'. The team should then agree exit criteria with the sponsor: the evidence that would end the project at each stage. Cooper (1990, p. 50) calls these 'preestablished criteria'. Holgersson et al. (2026) show the same for partnerships: preset criteria make it easier to close a collaboration without it looking like failure. Finally, a premortem (Klein, 2007) tells the team that the project 'has failed spectacularly' and has each member write down why. Had FedEx done this, 'no retailer will pay' and 'cities will not permit it' could have been on the page in 2019. Output: a signed brief with measures and exit conditions.

**Develop: build cheaply and test the riskiest assumption first.** The team should prototype to learn, not to impress. The first prototypes should take days and test the assumption most likely to kill the idea, which is usually demand and not engineering. The carrier could offer retailers a 'robot delivery' option that is fulfilled by a person with a trolley, to see whether anyone pays before a robot exists. Blank (2013, p. 68) calls this customer validation: testing interest 'through early orders or product usage'. Zipline's winch prototype failed in wind at its test site, and the team deleted the code for a faster approach because it 'looks scary' to the people receiving a delivery (Ackerman, 2024). Decisions should stay reversible while evidence is thin, which is the unit's distinction between two-way and one-way doors. A national unveiling is close to a one-way door, and FedEx showed its prototype on television before any market test (FedEx, 2019). Blank (2013, p. 68) notes that 'constant feedback yields better results than cadenced unveilings'. 'Fail fast' suits software better than machines on public streets, so safety-critical tests belong on private sites first. Output: evidence that moves items from assumed to known, or a decision to return to Define.

**Deliver: start narrow with paying customers and scale by gates.** The carrier should launch in one town with one retailer that pays. Zipline's first P2 customer delivery was in Pea Ridge, Arkansas (Sequoia Capital, 2025), and when Walmart signed for 300 more sites the two companies had only 21 active ones (Korosec, 2026a). Funding should be released in stages against the measures agreed in Define. Cooper's (1990, p. 46) gates end in a 'Go/Kill/Hold/Recycle decision', and Osterwalder et al. (2020, p. 17) reserve 'follow-up investments' for projects that provide real evidence. The team should track all three lenses, including cost per delivery, the gap that remains in Zipline's own case. Kaplan and Mikes (2012) separate preventable, strategy and external risks. Regulation here is an external risk, so the carrier should plan scenarios and track early warning signs. Finally, drivers and depot managers may resist automation, so they should be involved early. Lessons from the first town should start a new round of Discover. Output: a go, hold or stop decision at each gate, made on evidence.

### Individual behaviour recommendations

A process works only if individuals feed it honest information. Six behaviours matter most.

**1. Fall in love with the problem.** Every employee on an innovation project should be able to state, in one sentence, whose problem it solves. This is the simplest defence against the first of the unit's five pitfalls, working on the wrong problem. Zipline's chief executive shows the attitude when he says that 'the vehicle is only 15% of the complexity of the problem' (Sequoia Capital, 2025). A practical routine is to spend time each month with customers or front-line staff.

**2. Separate what is known from what is assumed.** Innovators become attached to their ideas and read weak signals as proof. The habit to build is asking 'what is the evidence, and who has checked it?' My audit internship is my reference point. In the audit of Liaoning Jincheng Petrochemical before its asset restructuring, a number in the accounts was a claim until it had been checked against records, and it was not accepted simply because management stated it. Roxo's central claim, that nearby customers would create demand for robot delivery, was a plausible statistic, and I found no report that it was tested with a paying retailer.

**3. Speak up early, especially with bad news.** Edmondson (1999, p. 354) defines psychological safety as 'a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking', and found across 51 teams that safer teams showed more learning behaviour. Voice is also an individual choice. Vuori and Huy (2016) found that Nokia's middle managers understood the smartphone threat but softened it for their superiors. Each employee should practise reporting problems plainly and early, with evidence and a proposed next step.

**4. Experiment small and treat failure as data.** Employees should prefer a test they can run this week to a plan they can perfect this quarter. The unit's advice is to make failure cheap, legible and classified: small enough to afford, written down so that others learn, and labelled so that a failed experiment is not treated like negligence. When a deliberately injected fault ended in a parachute landing, Zipline's engineers submitted improved code within 17 hours and later published the analysis (Watson, 2025).

**5. Question rules that may not exist, and build allies.** Large firms run on procedure. In ICBC's international business department I learned the steps for corporate cross-border remittances, where most steps exist for sound legal reasons. That experience sharpens the distinction: innovators should ask which rules are legal requirements and which are only habits, the orthodoxies of the fourth pitfall. They should also recruit sponsors and colleagues in other functions, because the fifth pitfall is failing to build coalitions. A marketing team, like the one I joined at SIASUN, is a natural ally because it carries customers' views back to engineers.

**6. Seek out people who think differently.** A problem has more sides than one person can see. Innovators should invite colleagues from operations, finance, legal and customer service into early discussions and treat disagreement about the task as useful. Belbin (2004, p. 10) found that of 25 teams built from the most able managers, only three won, which is why his team roles stress a mix of contributions.

These behaviours have a limit. Schein (1984) locates culture in shared basic assumptions, not espoused values, and notes that every group learns 'what gets rewarded' and 'what gets punished' (p. 11). Individuals can start these habits, but they last only if leaders make them safe.

@@leadership
### Leadership recommendations

Senior leaders decide whether the process and behaviours above survive contact with the budget. Six actions follow from the cases.

**1. Ring-fence exploration.** Because the core's returns are nearer and more certain, it tends to win contests for resources (March, 1991), and capital budgeting favours investments whose cash flow 'can be confidently projected' (Teece, 2007, p. 1327). Leaders should therefore set an explicit portfolio, for example a 70-20-10 split, and protect the exploratory share for a fixed period that cannot be raided in a difficult quarter. The split is only a rule of thumb; what matters is the protection. Roxo was cut about two weeks after cost savings were announced (FedEx, 2022; Contreras, 2022). Zipline's leaders stayed with P2 although its first delivery came about a year later than planned (Bellan, 2023), knowing that its failure would end the company (Sequoia Capital, 2025).

**2. Separate the unit and integrate at the top.** O'Reilly and Tushman (2004) found that more than 90% of breakthrough efforts succeeded when run as separate units, each with 'its own processes, structures, and cultures' but 'integrated into the existing senior management hierarchy'. None succeeded as independent units outside that hierarchy. The carrier should give its autonomy unit its own team and measures but put its head on the senior team, with two named sponsors.

**3. Fund in stages against evidence agreed in advance.** Protection must not become a blank cheque. Leaders should release money at gates tied to the measures in the Define brief (Cooper, 1990; Osterwalder et al., 2020) and close projects that miss them. Cooper (1990, p. 50) warns that without gates projects become 'express trains' that very little can stop. Roxo's cancellation was probably correct, but a gate in 2020 that required one paying retailer could have reached the same answer two years earlier.

**4. Reward bad news.** Leaders set the price of honesty. If the first person to report a failed test is blamed, reporting stops, as it did at Nokia (Vuori and Huy, 2016). Leaders should ask for problems in every review, respond with curiosity and describe their own mistakes. Because culture lies in basic assumptions and not in espoused values (Schein, 1984), posters and value statements will not achieve this; what changes behaviour is who is promoted and what happens to the bearer of bad news. The evidence here deserves care. Google's Project Aristotle, often cited for psychological safety, was never peer reviewed. Edmondson (1999) offers peer-reviewed support, although her design 'prevented a demonstration of causality' (p. 379).

**5. Engage regulators and communities as partners.** In delivery automation the right to operate is granted by others. Leaders should meet regulators before launch, publish safety data and listen to residents. Zipline had seven years of operations behind it when it unveiled P2 (Zipline, 2023), and later that year its first-generation drone earned FAA approval to fly beyond visual line of sight around Salt Lake City (FAA, 2023). FedEx received a cease-and-desist order (Korosec, 2019).

**6. Measure what you want more of.** 3M sets a target for the share of sales that must come from recently introduced products (Tidd and Bessant, 2021, p. 44), which keeps renewal on the agenda. For early projects the carrier should count leading indicators: assumptions tested, time to the first paying customer and projects stopped at a gate.

**Costs and trade-offs.** These actions are not free. Ring-fencing lowers short-term margins, and leaders must defend that to investors. The carrier must also stop some familiar habits: announcing projects before there is evidence, and funding exploration through the same annual budget round as the core. Stage gates need the discipline to stop popular projects. A separate unit can also become so isolated that it fails to draw on the core's resources, as happened at USA Today's website (O'Reilly and Tushman, 2004). Nor do these actions guarantee success. Roxo might have failed even with a perfect process, because the sector was immature. They do make it likely that failure is found early and cheaply.

@@conclusion
## Conclusion

This report defined innovation as a new idea that people actually use and that brings enough value to keep going, and the two cases support that test. Zipline defined a user problem, prototyped cheaply and protected a risky exploration until customers confirmed it, although its economics remain unproven. FedEx publicised a prototype, published no measure of what success meant, and ended the work under a cost programme. The difference lay less in technology than in management, and most of all in the Define stage.

For an established organisation the recommendations reduce to three commitments. Define the problem and the conditions for success and exit before building. Test with cheap prototypes and paying customers before publicity. Give exploration protected, staged funding with senior sponsorship. None of these guarantees success. Together they make it likely that a good idea reaches its users and that a weak one is stopped early at low cost.
